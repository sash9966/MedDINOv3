The Core Problem

MedDINOv3 was pretrained on CT-3M, a broad CT corpus dominated by non-contrast abdominal/chest scans and normalized with ImageNet statistics. Your dataset is cardiac CTA (CECT) — contrast-enhanced, where the blood pool, vessel walls, and cardiac chambers are 200–600 HU brighter than they would be on the pretraining distribution. The model's feature manifold was never exposed to contrast-enhanced anatomy, so:

  • Features that should fire on "aortic wall" fire on "dense soft tissue"
  • Intra-cardiac structure boundaries (ASD, VSD, valve anatomy) are invisible to the encoder
  • The model generalises to gross anatomy (ribs, lung, kidney outline) but not CHD-specific substructure

The Three Avenues

1
Segmentation Fine-tuning (Current Setup)
nnUNet + frozen/unfrozen MedDINOv3 backbone · supervised on labeled CECT
Running now

The Primus decoder learns to map MedDINOv3's pretrained features to segmentation labels. The backbone can be partially or fully unfrozen so that features shift toward CECT anatomy. This is the cheapest intervention and already running on Sherlock.

Pros

  • Already running — no extra engineering
  • Supervised signal directly targets the structures you care about
  • Even a frozen backbone with an adapted decoder can partially close the domain gap
  • nnUNet handles class imbalance, augmentation, patch scheduling automatically
  • 100 labeled images can work with strong pretraining if anatomy is consistent

Cons

  • Domain shift is never truly fixed — encoder features remain NCCT-biased
  • 100 labeled volumes is marginal for CHD substructure (VSD, AVSD, valve morphology vary enormously)
  • Backbone unfreezing with few labels risks catastrophic forgetting of pretrained structure
  • No cross-slice context in the 2D model — each slice processed independently
  • CECT HU shift means early-layer patch embeddings are off from day 1
Key risk with 100 images
CHD labels vary enormously by defect subtype (TOF, TGA, AVSD, etc.). 100 volumes will likely have thin coverage across subtypes. The model may learn the dominant anatomy but fail on rare variants — exactly the cases where clinical value is highest.

2
Full 3D DINO Pretraining from MedDINOv3 Init
Continue SSL pretraining on CECT using 3D inflated weights · random 3D masking
Feasible at 2K scale

Start from the inflated 3D MedDINOv3 checkpoint and continue DINO/iBOT self-supervised pretraining on your 2,000 CECT volumes. The model learns to reconstruct masked 3D patches, forcing it to build a CECT-specific, volumetrically-aware feature space. The depth positional encoding gets trained instead of being random.

Pros

  • Directly solves the HU domain gap at the encoder level
  • Trains the depth positional encoding (currently random/contaminated)
  • True 3D cross-slice attention — learns volumetric structure
  • 2,000 unlabeled CECT volumes are sufficient to continue SSL pretraining
  • Initializing from inflated weights (not random) dramatically reduces data needed vs. from scratch
  • Features would be CECT-native before any segmentation fine-tuning

Cons

  • Computationally very expensive — 3D ViT-B on full cardiac CTA volumes
  • Memory: full cardiac CTA at native resolution won't fit; needs aggressive patching
  • Random 3D masking does not know about anatomy — may mask too much background (air, non-cardiac structures)
  • Significant engineering to extend 2D DINO training pipeline to 3D
  • 2,000 volumes is the lower bound; MedDINOv3 itself used ~10,000+ volume-equivalents
  • Risk of forgetting NCCT structural priors if learning rate too high
The memory wall
A full cardiac CTA is typically 512×512×300+. At patch_size=(2,16,16), that is ~150,000 tokens. ViT-B self-attention is O(n²) — this is infeasible at full resolution. You would need to crop to cardiac ROI (~200×200×150), use larger d_patch (4–8), or switch to a windowed/local attention variant. Each of these is a non-trivial design choice.

3
Anatomy-Guided Masked Pretraining
TotalSegmentator masks · structure-aware DINO masking · cardiac-focused SSL
Most novel · high payoff

Run TotalSegmentator (or a cardiac-specific segmentor) to generate pseudo-labels for anatomical structures (aorta, PA, RA/LA/RV/LV, valves). Use these masks to guide the DINO masking strategy: instead of masking random patches, mask entire anatomical substructures and force the model to reconstruct them from context. This teaches the encoder that different cardiac substructures are semantically distinct entities — not just "dense tissue at location X."

Pros

  • Directly encodes anatomical knowledge into the SSL objective
  • Forces the model to separate aorta from PA from chambers in feature space
  • Can work in 2D (per-slice masking) — no memory wall from Avenue 2
  • Pseudo-labels are free (TotalSegmentator takes ~30s per volume on GPU)
  • More data-efficient than random masking — each masked patch carries more signal
  • Most directly addresses your goal: feature separation by cardiac substructure
  • Works well even with 500–1,000 unlabeled CECT volumes
  • Novel research contribution — anatomy-guided DINO masking is not well explored

Cons

  • TotalSegmentator is trained on "normal" anatomy — CHD patients have abnormal structure; pseudo-labels may be wrong precisely where it matters most
  • Masking strategy design is non-trivial: mask one structure vs. multiple? mask 50% of structure or all of it?
  • Engineering: need to modify the DINO data pipeline to accept anatomical masks
  • Possible mode collapse: model learns "mask = structure boundary" as a shortcut
  • Requires running TotalSegmentator at scale on 2,000 volumes (compute cost)
  • Validation is harder — hard to know if features improved without downstream eval
Why this is the most interesting research angle

Standard DINO masking treats every patch as equally informative. In cardiac CTA, the aortic root and the right ventricular outflow tract are adjacent spatially but completely distinct clinically. Anatomy-guided masking encodes this prior directly: "reconstruct the aortic arch from the surrounding context" is a much harder and more clinically meaningful pretext task than "reconstruct a random 16×16 patch." If this works, the CLS token and patch tokens will naturally cluster by cardiac substructure without any label supervision — exactly what you need for few-shot CHD segmentation.

The CHD anatomy problem
TotalSegmentator will fail on the most severe CHD cases — transposition, single ventricle, heterotaxy. These are exactly the cases with the highest clinical complexity. Mitigation: use TotalSegmentator only on "simpler" cases (ASD, VSD, mild coarctation) for pretraining, then fine-tune with manual labels on complex cases. Or train a CHD-specific rough segmentor first using your 100 labeled scans.

Recommended Path

1
Now (0–3 months): Run Avenue 1 to completion on your 100 labeled images. This gives you a segmentation baseline and tells you how bad the domain gap actually is in practice. Evaluate per-structure Dice across CHD subtypes — if you see >0.7 Dice on the major structures, the pretrained features are transferring well enough to continue.
Key experiment: compare frozen encoder vs. unfrozen at epochs 50–100. If unfreezing hurts, the pretrained features are stable; if it helps, domain adaptation is needed.
2
Near-term (when 2,000 images arrive): Pursue Avenue 3 first, not Avenue 2. Run TotalSegmentator on all 2,000 volumes (batch overnight on Sherlock). Use the pseudo-labels for anatomy-guided SSL pretraining in 2D — this is lower engineering cost than full 3D and higher payoff than random masking. Train for 50–100 epochs from the MedDINOv3 2D checkpoint.
Exclude severe CHD outliers from TotalSeg masking (flag by checking if TotalSeg cardiac volume is implausible). Use those cases only in the supervised fine-tuning stage.
3
Longer term (3–6 months, if compute allows): Attempt Avenue 2 using the anatomy-guided checkpoint from step 2 as init (not the random inflated weights). Now the depth positional encoding can be fine-tuned with a meaningful feature base, and 3D cross-slice context can close the last gap.
The Avenue 2 → 3 ordering matters enormously. Doing 3D SSL from a semantically meaningful 2D init will converge much faster and require far less data than from the inflation-only init.
Bottom line on your 200-image question:

200 labeled images can work for the segmentation fine-tune (Avenue 1) if your CHD subtype distribution is reasonably uniform — nnUNet's patch-based training and MedDINOv3's pretrained priors help a lot. But 200 is not enough to reliably learn rare variant anatomy from scratch. The 2,000-image CECT dataset changes everything: it unlocks Avenue 3 (the most promising path) and turns your labeled fine-tuning from marginal to very strong. The 2,000-image arrival is the key milestone to plan your research around.
